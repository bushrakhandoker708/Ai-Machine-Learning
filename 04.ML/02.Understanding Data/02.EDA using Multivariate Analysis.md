## Definitions

| Level            | Variables at once | Question                                | Example                                                                |
| ---------------- | ----------------- | --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Univariate**   | 1                 | What does this column look like?        | Distribution of `total_bill`                                           |
| **Bivariate**    | 2                 | Are these two columns related, and how? | Does `tip` rise with `total_bill`?                                     |
| **Multivariate** | 3 or more         | How do several columns interact?        | How do bill, day, smoker status and party size together shape the tip? |

Univariate analysis ([[06.Univariate Analysis]]) told you what each column looks like. Bivariate and multivariate analysis answer the questions that actually matter in most projects:

- Which features are related to the **target**? (feature selection)
- Which features are related to **each other**? (redundancy, multicollinearity)
- Do **groups** differ? (segmentation, A/B comparisons)
- Is a relationship **changed by a third variable**? (interaction, confounding)

## The decision map: which analysis for which pair?

This is the most important table in the note. First classify both variables (numerical or categorical), then pick.

|X \ Y|**Numerical**|**Categorical**|
|---|---|---|
|**Numerical**|Scatter plot, correlation (Pearson/Spearman), regression line, hexbin, line plot (if X is time)|Box / violin / KDE of the numeric variable split by category; `barplot` of means|
|**Categorical**|Box / violin / strip / bar of the numeric variable by category|Crosstab, grouped count plot, stacked bar, heatmap, chi-square test|

|Pair|Visual|Summary numbers|Test (optional)|
|---|---|---|---|
|Num vs Num|scatter, `regplot`, `jointplot`, hexbin|Pearson r, Spearman rho, Kendall tau|test of correlation (`pearsonr` p-value)|
|Num vs Cat (2 groups)|box, violin, KDE overlay|mean/median per group, difference|t-test, Mann-Whitney U|
|Num vs Cat (3+ groups)|box, violin, bar with CI|mean/median per group|ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis|
|Cat vs Cat|crosstab heatmap, stacked/grouped bars|row percentages, group means of a 0/1 target|chi-square, Cramér's V|
|Num vs Time|line plot|rolling mean, growth rate|trend and seasonality checks|

##  Correlation is not causation

Two variables moving together does not mean one causes the other.

- **Confounding:** a third variable drives both (ice-cream sales and drowning both rise in summer).
- **Reverse causation:** the arrow runs the other way.
- **Coincidence:** with many columns, some pairs correlate by chance.
- **Selection effects:** the sample itself creates the pattern.

Bivariate analysis **suggests hypotheses**. Experiments (A/B tests) or causal methods are needed to prove them.

# Numerical vs numerical

## B1. Scatter plot (`sns.scatterplot`)

Each row becomes a point on an (X, Y) plane. You look for **direction, shape, strength, and outliers**.

```python
sns.scatterplot(data=tips, x="total_bill", y="tip")
plt.title("Tip vs total bill")
plt.show()
```

**Reading it:** the points rise from lower left to upper right, a clear **positive, roughly linear** relationship. Bigger bills tend to get bigger tips. The cloud also fans out as the bill grows (larger spread at high bills), a pattern called **heteroscedasticity**.

### What to look for in any scatter plot

|Feature|Meaning|
|---|---|
|Upward slope|positive relationship|
|Downward slope|negative relationship|
|Tight band around a line|strong relationship|
|Wide cloud|weak relationship|
|Curve (U, S, exponential)|non-linear; Pearson r understates it|
|Fan or funnel shape|variance changes with X|
|Separate clusters|hidden subgroups (use `hue`)|
|Isolated points|outliers or errors|
|Horizontal or vertical stripes|discrete or rounded variable|

## B2. Adding dimensions: `hue`, `style`, `size`

A single scatter plot can encode up to **five variables**:

|Argument|Encodes|Variable type|
|---|---|---|
|`x`|axis 1|numerical|
|`y`|axis 2|numerical|
|`hue`|colour|categorical (or numerical)|
|`style`|marker shape|categorical|
|`size`|marker size|numerical or ordinal|

```python
sns.scatterplot(
    data=tips,
    x="total_bill", y="tip",
    hue="sex",            # 3rd variable: colour
    style="smoker",       # 4th variable: marker shape
    size="size",          # 5th variable: party size
    sizes=(30, 250),
    alpha=0.8,
)
plt.legend(bbox_to_anchor=(1.02, 1), loc="upper left")
plt.show()
```

Reading it: colour separates male and female diners, marker shape separates smokers from non-smokers, and larger markers are larger parties. Bigger parties cluster toward larger bills, which also have larger tips.

> **Practical limit:** beyond three or four encodings a plot becomes hard to read. Use facets (Part E) for extra variables.

## B3. Quantifying the relationship: correlation

```python
tips[["total_bill", "tip"]].corr()                    # Pearson r
tips["total_bill"].corr(tips["tip"])                  # 0.676
tips["total_bill"].corr(tips["tip"], method="spearman")
tips["total_bill"].corr(tips["tip"], method="kendall")
```

|Coefficient|Measures|Use when|
|---|---|---|
|**Pearson r**|**linear** association|roughly linear, no heavy outliers, numeric|
|**Spearman rho**|**monotonic** association (ranks)|non-linear but consistently rising or falling, outliers, ordinal data|
|**Kendall tau**|rank agreement|small samples, many ties|

| |r| | Rough strength | |---|---| | 0.0 to 0.2 | negligible | | 0.2 to 0.4 | weak | | 0.4 to 0.6 | moderate | | 0.6 to 0.8 | strong | | 0.8 to 1.0 | very strong |

The sign gives the direction. `r = 0.68` between bill and tip is a strong positive linear relationship. `r` squared (`0.46`) is the share of variance in tip explained linearly by the bill.

### Significance test for a correlation

```python
r, p = stats.pearsonr(tips["total_bill"], tips["tip"])
print(f"r = {r:.3f}, p = {p:.2e}")
```

A tiny p-value says the correlation is unlikely to be exactly zero in the population, not that it is large or meaningful.

### Correlation matrix and heatmap

```python
corr = tips.corr(numeric_only=True)
sns.heatmap(corr, annot=True, fmt=".2f", cmap="coolwarm", vmin=-1, vmax=1, square=True)
plt.title("Correlation matrix (tips)")
plt.show()
```

`numeric_only=True` is required in pandas 2.x because the dataset contains text and category columns.

Cleaner, with the redundant upper triangle masked:

```python
mask = np.triu(np.ones_like(corr, dtype=bool))
sns.heatmap(corr, mask=mask, annot=True, fmt=".2f", cmap="coolwarm", vmin=-1, vmax=1,
            square=True, linewidths=0.5)
plt.show()
```

## B4. Fitting a line: `regplot` and `lmplot`

```python
sns.regplot(data=tips, x="total_bill", y="tip", scatter_kws={"alpha": 0.5})
plt.show()

# One regression line per group
sns.lmplot(data=tips, x="total_bill", y="tip", hue="smoker", height=5, aspect=1.2)

# Separate panels

```

The shaded band is a 95% confidence interval around the fitted line.

Numeric slope and intercept:

```python
res = stats.linregress(tips["total_bill"], tips["tip"])
print(f"tip is about {res.intercept:.2f} + {res.slope:.3f} x total_bill  (r2 = {res.rvalue**2:.2f})")
# roughly: each extra $10 on the bill adds about $1.05 in tip
```

### Residual plot: is a straight line appropriate?

```python
sns.residplot(data=tips, x="total_bill", y="tip", lowess=True)
plt.axhline(0, color="red", ls="--")
plt.show()
```

Residuals should look like random noise around zero. A curve or a funnel signals non-linearity or changing variance.

## B5. Useful derived variable: ratios

Raw columns can hide the real story. A ratio can be a better target:

```python
tips["tip_pct"] = tips["tip"] / tips["total_bill"] * 100
tips["tip_pct"].describe().round(1)                 # mean about 16%
sns.scatterplot(data=tips, x="total_bill", y="tip_pct")
```

The scatter of tip percent against bill is nearly flat (or slightly falling): people leave about the same percentage regardless of the bill, which is why the raw tip rises with the bill.

## B7. Too many points? Overplotting

With thousands of rows, points hide each other.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
big = pd.DataFrame({"x": rng.normal(size=50_000)})
big["y"] = 0.6 * big["x"] + rng.normal(scale=0.8, size=50_000)

plt.hexbin(big["x"], big["y"], gridsize=40, cmap="viridis", mincnt=1); plt.colorbar(label="count")
plt.show()

sns.kdeplot(data=big.sample(5000, random_state=0), x="x", y="y", fill=True)   # density contours
sns.scatterplot(data=big.sample(2000, random_state=0), x="x", y="y", alpha=0.2, s=8)
```

Remedies: transparency (`alpha`), smaller markers, sampling, hexbin, 2D KDE, or `sns.histplot(x=, y=)` for a 2D histogram.

## B8. Line plot (`sns.lineplot`)

A scatter plot whose points are **connected in order**. Use it when X is **time** (or any ordered variable) and you want the trend.

```python
yearly = flights.groupby("year", as_index=False)["passengers"].sum()
sns.lineplot(data=yearly, x="year", y="passengers", marker="o")
plt.title("Total passengers per year")
plt.show()
```

Total passengers grew steadily from 1,520 in 1949 to 5,714 in 1960, roughly a 3.8x increase.

### Seaborn aggregates automatically

If several rows share the same X, `lineplot` draws the mean and a 95% confidence band:

```python
sns.lineplot(data=flights, x="year", y="passengers")        # mean of 12 months per year, with CI band
sns.lineplot(data=flights, x="year", y="passengers", estimator="sum", errorbar=None)
sns.lineplot(data=flights, x="year", y="passengers", hue="month")   # one line per month
```

`ci=` is deprecated; use `errorbar=` (for example `errorbar=("ci", 95)` or `errorbar=None`).

### Time-series extras

```python
f = flights.copy()
f["date"] = pd.to_datetime(f["year"].astype(str) + "-" + f["month"].astype(str), format="%Y-%b")
s = f.set_index("date")["passengers"]

s.plot(label="monthly")
s.rolling(12).mean().plot(label="12-month average (trend)")
plt.legend(); plt.show()

s.pct_change(12).dropna().plot(title="Year-over-year growth")
```

A 12-month rolling mean removes the seasonal wiggle and exposes the trend. Notice that the seasonal swings **grow** with the level, so the pattern is multiplicative; a log transform makes it additive.

## B9. When correlation lies: Anscombe's quartet

Four datasets with almost identical mean, variance, and correlation (r about 0.82) but completely different shapes.

```python
anscombe = sns.load_dataset("anscombe")
print(anscombe.groupby("dataset").agg(x_mean=("x", "mean"), y_mean=("y", "mean"),
                                       r=("x", lambda s: s.corr(anscombe.loc[s.index, "y"]))).round(2))

sns.lmplot(data=anscombe, x="x", y="y", col="dataset", col_wrap=2, height=3, ci=None)
plt.show()
```

One is linear, one is a clean curve, one has a single outlier dragging the line, one is a vertical stack with one leverage point. **Always plot before trusting a coefficient.**

## B10. Outliers, non-linearity, and Spearman

```python
x = np.arange(1, 51)
y_curve = np.exp(x / 10)                       # monotonic but non-linear
print("Pearson :", round(np.corrcoef(x, y_curve)[0, 1], 2))                 # below 1
print("Spearman:", round(stats.spearmanr(x, y_curve).correlation, 2))       # 1.0

y_out = x + np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 3, 50); y_out[-1] = -200       # one wild point
print("Pearson with outlier :", round(np.corrcoef(x, y_out)[0, 1], 2))
print("Spearman with outlier:", round(stats.spearmanr(x, y_out).correlation, 2))
```

Spearman is much less affected by outliers and captures any monotonic trend.

## B11. Simpson's paradox: the third variable can reverse the story

In `iris`, the overall relation between sepal length and sepal width is slightly **negative**, but **within each species** it is **positive**.

```python
print("overall r:", round(iris["sepal_length"].corr(iris["sepal_width"]), 2))      # about -0.12
print(iris.groupby("species")[["sepal_length", "sepal_width"]]
          .apply(lambda g: g["sepal_length"].corr(g["sepal_width"])).round(2))   # about +0.74, +0.53, +0.46

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
sns.regplot(data=iris, x="sepal_length", y="sepal_width", ax=axes[0], scatter_kws={"alpha": 0.5})
axes[0].set_title("Pooled: slightly negative")
sns.scatterplot(data=iris, x="sepal_length", y="sepal_width", hue="species", ax=axes[1])
for sp, g in iris.groupby("species"):
    m, b = np.polyfit(g["sepal_length"], g["sepal_width"], 1)
    xs = np.linspace(g["sepal_length"].min(), g["sepal_length"].max(), 10)
    axes[1].plot(xs, m * xs + b)
axes[1].set_title("By species: positive in each")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

The classic medical version, with real numbers:

```python
stones = pd.DataFrame({
    "treatment": ["A", "A", "B", "B"],
    "stone":     ["small", "large", "small", "large"],
    "success":   [81, 192, 234, 55],
    "total":     [87, 263, 270, 80],
})
stones["rate"] = (stones["success"] / stones["total"]).round(2)

by_stone = stones                                                    # A wins in BOTH groups (0.93 vs 0.87, 0.73 vs 0.69)
overall = stones.groupby("treatment")[["success", "total"]].sum()
overall["rate"] = (overall["success"] / overall["total"]).round(2)   # but B looks better overall (0.83 vs 0.78)
```

Treatment A is better for small stones and for large stones, yet B looks better in total, because A was given mostly to the harder (large-stone) cases. **Lesson:** a pooled relationship can reverse once you split by a lurking variable. Always check by key groups.

**Goal:** compare a numeric variable (its centre, spread, shape) across groups.

## C1. Group summaries first (always)

```python
titanic.groupby("pclass")["fare"].agg(["count", "mean", "median", "std", "min", "max"]).round(2)
```

|pclass|count|mean|median|
|---|---|---|---|
|1|216|84.15|60.29|
|2|184|20.66|14.25|
|3|491|13.68|8.05|

```python
tips.groupby("day")["tip"].agg(["count", "mean", "median"]).round(2)
tips.groupby(["sex", "smoker"])["tip"].mean().unstack().round(2)
```

Use `agg` with named outputs for tidy tables:

## C2. Bar plot (`sns.barplot`): mean per category

```python
sns.barplot(data=titanic, x="pclass", y="fare")
plt.title("Average fare by passenger class")
plt.show()
```

First-class passengers paid far more on average (about 84) than second (about 21) or third class (about 14).

### The black lines are confidence intervals

Each bar shows the **mean**, and the black line is a **95% confidence interval** for that mean, calculated by bootstrapping. A short line means the mean is estimated precisely; a long line means high uncertainty (few rows or high variability). Non-overlapping intervals suggest a real difference.

```python
sns.barplot(data=titanic, x="pclass", y="fare", errorbar=("ci", 95))   # default
sns.barplot(data=titanic, x="pclass", y="fare", errorbar="sd")         # show spread of the data instead
sns.barplot(data=titanic, x="pclass", y="fare", errorbar=None)         # no interval
```

In seaborn versions before 0.12 the argument was `ci=`; it is deprecated now.

### Choose another statistic

```python
sns.barplot(data=titanic, x="pclass", y="fare", estimator=np.median)
sns.barplot(data=titanic, x="pclass", y="survived")        # mean of a 0/1 column = survival rate
```

> A bar plot of **means** hides distributions. A mean of 84 for first class does not show that some fares are 512. Pair it with a box or violin plot.

Within each class, female passengers paid higher average fares than males, most visibly in first class.

## C5. Distribution overlays: KDE (and histograms) by category

To compare **whole distributions** rather than a single summary, overlay the probability density functions.

> `sns.distplot` is removed in recent seaborn. Use `kdeplot`, `histplot`, or `displot` with `hue`.

```python
sns.kdeplot(data=titanic, x="age", hue="survived", fill=True, common_norm=False, alpha=0.4)
plt.title("Age distribution: died (0) vs survived (1)")
plt.show()
```

`common_norm=False` normalises each group separately so that you compare **shapes** rather than letting the larger group dominate.

### Reading the plot: "children first"

- **Under about 15:** the survived curve is **higher** than the died curve. Children had a higher probability of surviving than dying.
- **About 15 to 30:** the died curve is **higher**. Young adults, especially men, were more likely to die.
- **Over about 60:** survival curve is low; older passengers fared poorly.
- Both curves peak in the 20s because that is where most passengers are.

Prove it with numbers (a common bivariate step is to bin the numeric variable):

```python
titanic["age_group"] = pd.cut(titanic["age"], bins=[0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 80],
                              labels=["0-15", "15-30", "30-45", "45-60", "60-80"])
titanic.groupby("age_group", observed=True)["survived"].agg(["mean", "count"]).round(3)
```

Survival is highest for the 0-15 group (above 50%) and lowest among older passengers.

### Other overlay styles

## C6. Is the difference real? Statistical tests

Plots suggest, tests quantify the evidence.

### Two groups

```python
fare_surv = titanic.loc[titanic["survived"] == 1, "fare"]
fare_died = titanic.loc[titanic["survived"] == 0, "fare"]

print("means  :", fare_surv.mean().round(1), fare_died.mean().round(1))      # about 48.4 vs 22.1
print("Welch t:", stats.ttest_ind(fare_surv, fare_died, equal_var=False))
print("Mann-Whitney U:", stats.mannwhitneyu(fare_surv, fare_died))
```

|Test|Assumes|Use when|
|---|---|---|
|**Welch t-test**|roughly normal means, not equal variance|decent sample size, mild skew|
|**Mann-Whitney U**|no normality needed|skewed data (like `fare`), ordinal data|

### Three or more groups

```python
groups = [g["fare"].values for _, g in titanic.groupby("pclass")]
print("ANOVA   :", stats.f_oneway(*groups))
print("Kruskal :", stats.kruskal(*groups))        # non-parametric alternative
```

After a significant ANOVA, find **which** groups differ with a post-hoc test (Tukey HSD):

```python
from statsmodels.stats.multicomp import pairwise_tukeyhsd
print(pairwise_tukeyhsd(titanic["fare"], titanic["pclass"]))
```

### Effect size: how big is the difference?

```python
def cohens_d(a, b):
    na, nb = len(a), len(b)
    pooled = np.sqrt(((na - 1) * a.var(ddof=1) + (nb - 1) * b.var(ddof=1)) / (na + nb - 2))
    return (a.mean() - b.mean()) / pooled

print("Cohen's d:", round(cohens_d(fare_surv, fare_died), 2))     # about 0.5 (medium)

def eta_squared(df, value, group):
    grand = df[value].mean()
    ss_between = sum(len(g) * (g[value].mean() - grand) ** 2 for _, g in df.groupby(group))
    ss_total = ((df[value] - grand) ** 2).sum()
    return ss_between / ss_total

print("eta^2 (fare by pclass):", round(eta_squared(titanic, "fare", "pclass"), 3))
```

|Cohen's d|Size|
|---|---|
|0.2|small|
|0.5|medium|
|0.8|large|

A tiny p-value with a tiny effect size is statistically significant but practically unimportant; with large samples, nearly everything is significant. Report **both**.


# Part D: Categorical vs categorical

**Goal:** see whether the distribution of one category depends on another; compare **proportions**, not just counts.

## D1. Cross-tabulation (`pd.crosstab`)

A **contingency table**: rows are one variable, columns the other, cells count rows.

```python
pd.crosstab(titanic["pclass"], titanic["survived"])
```

|pclass|died (0)|survived (1)|
|---|---|---|
|1|80|136|
|2|97|87|
|3|372|119|

```python
pd.crosstab(titanic["pclass"], titanic["survived"], margins=True)             # add totals
pd.crosstab(titanic["pclass"], titanic["survived"], normalize="index")        # row percentages
pd.crosstab(titanic["pclass"], titanic["survived"], normalize="columns")      # column percentages
```

## D5. Is the association real? Chi-square test and Cramér's V

```python
ct = pd.crosstab(titanic["sex"], titanic["survived"])
chi2, p, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(ct)
print(f"chi2 = {chi2:.1f}, dof = {dof}, p = {p:.2e}")
print(pd.DataFrame(expected, index=ct.index, columns=ct.columns).round(1))    # counts expected if independent
```

**Idea:** if the variables were independent, each cell would equal (row total x column total) / grand total. The chi-square statistic measures how far the observed counts are from those expectations. A small p-value means the variables are probably not independent.

Requirements: counts, not percentages; expected counts of at least about 5 in most cells.

### Effect size: Cramér's V

```python
def cramers_v(x, y):
    ct = pd.crosstab(x, y)
    chi2 = stats.chi2_contingency(ct, correction=False)[0]
    n = ct.values.sum()
    r, k = ct.shape
    return np.sqrt(chi2 / (n * (min(r, k) - 1)))

for col in ["sex", "pclass", "embarked", "alone"]:
    print(f"{col:9s} V = {cramers_v(titanic[col], titanic['survived']):.2f}")
```

|V|Association|
|---|---|
|0.0 to 0.1|negligible|
|0.1 to 0.3|weak|
|0.3 to 0.5|moderate|
|above 0.5|strong|

`sex` is the strongest (V about 0.54), `pclass` moderate (about 0.34), `embarked` weaker (about 0.17).

### Case 3: churn with a hidden confounder (synthetic)

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n = 6000
plan = rng.choice(["basic", "premium"], n, p=[0.7, 0.3])
segment = np.where(plan == "premium", rng.choice(["business", "consumer"], n, p=[0.8, 0.2]),
                                       rng.choice(["business", "consumer"], n, p=[0.2, 0.8]))
base = np.where(segment == "business", 0.05, 0.25)                 # business customers churn less
churn = rng.random(n) < base + np.where(plan == "premium", 0.03, 0.0)
cust = pd.DataFrame({"plan": plan, "segment": segment, "churn": churn.astype(int)})

print(cust.groupby("plan")["churn"].mean().round(3))                         # premium looks safer overall...
print(cust.groupby(["segment", "plan"])["churn"].mean().unstack().round(3))   # ...but premium churns MORE within each segment
```

Overall, premium looks like it retains customers better. Within each segment, premium actually churns slightly more. The segment (business vs consumer) was driving both plan choice and churn. A decision made from the pooled table (push everyone to premium to cut churn) would be wrong. This is Simpson's paradox in a business setting.

## F3. Automating bivariate analysis against a target

```python
def bivariate_vs_target(df, target, cat_max=12):
    """Rank every feature by its association with the target."""
    rows = []
    y = df[target]
    y_is_cat = (not pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(y)) or y.nunique() <= 2
    for col in df.columns.drop(target):
        x = df[col]
        sub = df[[col, target]].dropna()
        if len(sub) < 20 or sub[col].nunique() < 2:
            continue
        x_is_num = pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(x) and x.nunique() > cat_max
        if x_is_num and not y_is_cat:
            stat, p = stats.spearmanr(sub[col], sub[target]); kind, name = "num-num", "spearman"
        elif x_is_num and y_is_cat:
            groups = [g[col].values for _, g in sub.groupby(target)]
            stat, p = stats.kruskal(*groups); kind, name = "num-cat", "kruskal H"
        elif (not x_is_num) and y_is_cat:
            stat = cramers_v(sub[col], sub[target]); p = stats.chi2_contingency(pd.crosstab(sub[col], sub[target]))[1]
            kind, name = "cat-cat", "cramers V"
        else:
            groups = [g[target].values for _, g in sub.groupby(col)]
            stat, p = stats.kruskal(*groups); kind, name = "cat-num", "kruskal H"
        rows.append({"feature": col, "pair": kind, "measure": name, "value": round(float(stat), 3), "p_value": float(p)})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows).sort_values("p_value")

bivariate_vs_target(titanic.drop(columns=["alive"]), "survived")
```

Use it as a **screening tool**, not a verdict. Measures differ across pair types, so compare ranks within a type. Always follow up with plots.

### Quick plot helper by pair type

```python
def plot_pair(df, x, y, hue=None):
    xn = pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(df[x]) and df[x].nunique() > 12
    yn = pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(df[y]) and df[y].nunique() > 12
    if xn and yn:
        sns.scatterplot(data=df, x=x, y=y, hue=hue, alpha=0.6)
    elif xn != yn:
        num, cat = (x, y) if xn else (y, x)
        sns.boxplot(data=df, x=cat, y=num, hue=hue) if num == y else sns.boxplot(data=df, x=num, y=cat, hue=hue)
    else:
        sns.heatmap(pd.crosstab(df[y], df[x], normalize="columns") * 100, annot=True, fmt=".0f", cmap="Blues")
    plt.show()
```

## F4. Interpretation guide

|What you see|Meaning|Next step|
|---|---|---|
|Tight upward cloud (r above 0.7)|strong positive linear link|consider regression; check for multicollinearity|
|Curved cloud, Pearson low, Spearman high|monotonic non-linear|transform or use non-linear models|
|Fan shape|variance grows with X|log transform; weighted models|
|Separate clusters in a scatter|hidden groups|colour by candidate categories|
|Medians differ, boxes barely overlap|strong group difference|test and quantify effect size|
|Boxes overlap heavily|weak or no difference|likely drop or low priority|
|KDE curves cross|the favoured group changes with X|bin X and compute group rates|
|Row percentages differ a lot|association between categoricals|chi-square and Cramér's V|
|Overall trend flips when split|Simpson's paradox|stratify by the lurking variable|
|Parallel lines in an interaction plot|no interaction|additive model is fine|
|Crossing lines|interaction|add an interaction term or use trees|
|Two features correlated above 0.9|redundancy|drop or combine one|
|A feature with near-zero relation to the target|little predictive value|candidate for removal (but check interactions and non-linearity)|

## F5. Common mistakes and fixes

|Mistake|Why it hurts|Fix|
|---|---|---|
|Comparing counts across unequal groups|bias from group size|compare percentages or means|
|Reading correlation as causation|wrong decisions|confounders, experiments|
|Computing Pearson without plotting|Anscombe-type surprises|scatter first|
|Using Pearson for ordinal or skewed data|distorted by outliers|Spearman|
|Using `corr()` on text columns|error in pandas 2.x|`numeric_only=True`|
|Using `sns.distplot`|removed in new seaborn|`kdeplot`, `histplot`, `displot`|
|Bar plot of means only|hides distribution|add box or violin plot|
|Forgetting group sizes|tiny groups look dramatic|show `count` next to every rate|
|Ignoring missing values in either column|pairs silently dropped|check `dropna` effects; compare missing rate by group|
|Too many encodings in one plot|unreadable|facets|
|Overplotting thousands of points|hides density|`alpha`, hexbin, sampling|
|Running many tests without correction|false positives|Bonferroni / FDR, or treat as exploratory|
|Treating p-value as importance|big n makes trivial effects "significant"|report effect size|
|Skipping the third variable|Simpson's paradox|stratify|
|Using a heatmap of raw counts for unequal groups|misleading colours|normalise|
|Clustering with filled-in zeros|false patterns|mask low-count cells|
|Using target information to build features before splitting|leakage|split first, then explore on the training set|

## Key takeaways

- **Bivariate analysis** relates two variables; **multivariate analysis** looks at three or more together. Pick tools by variable types: scatter and correlation (num-num), bar/box/violin/KDE overlays (num-cat), crosstab and heatmap (cat-cat).
- A **scatter plot** with `hue`, `style` and `size` can show up to five variables; facets scale further. Line plots are scatter plots for ordered or time data.
- **Bar plots** show means with 95% confidence intervals (the black lines); pair them with box or violin plots to see the whole distribution. KDE overlays reveal where one group is more likely than another, such as children under 15 surviving more on the Titanic.
- For categoricals compare **percentages**: survival is about 62% in first class, 47% in second, 24% in third; about 74% for women and 19% for men.
- **Cluster maps** group categories with similar behaviour; **pair plots** reveal structure across all numeric pairs (iris species separate on petal measurements); **pivot-table heatmaps** expose trend and seasonality (flights: growth plus July/August peaks).
- Always **plot before trusting a number** (Anscombe), watch for **confounders** and **Simpson's paradox**, and distinguish significance from effect size.
- Univariate, bivariate, and multivariate findings feed straight into cleaning, feature engineering, and model choice. The next note covers automating all of this with profiling tools.
