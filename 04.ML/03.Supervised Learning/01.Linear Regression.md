
## 1. What Is Linear Regression, Actually?

Strip away the math for a second. You have a bunch of examples — inputs and a number you're trying to predict for each one. Linear regression makes one simple assumption: **the output is a weighted sum of the inputs, plus some constant offset.**

That's it. No curves, no interactions, no "if-then" logic — just: multiply each input by some weight, add them all up, add one more constant, and that's your prediction.

Why would such a simple assumption ever work? Because a huge number of real relationships are _approximately_ linear over the range you care about — price vs. square footage, calories vs. weight gain, ad spend vs. revenue (up to a point). And even when the true relationship isn't linear, linear regression becomes the first sanity-check model you fit before reaching for anything fancier.

It is also the most important model in this entire vault to understand _deeply_, because — as you'll see in Section 14 — every neural network's output layer is just linear regression wearing a costume.

### The Formal Setup

**Given:** a dataset ${(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, y^{(i)})}_{i=1}^n$ where each input $\mathbf{x}^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (a vector of $d$ numbers) and each output $y^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}$ (a single number).

**Goal:** learn parameters $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$\hat{y}^{(i)} = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}^{(i)} + b$$

Here $\mathbf{w}$ holds the **weights** (how much each feature matters) and $b$ is the **bias/intercept** (the baseline prediction when every feature is zero).

### A Notational Trick: Absorbing the Bias

Carrying $b$ around separately is annoying in matrix algebra. So we do a trick: glue a constant feature equal to 1 onto every input vector. Now the bias becomes just another weight:

$$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w}, \qquad X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (d+1)}, \qquad \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{d+1}$$

where $X$ stacks every training example as a row, with a column of 1s glued on:

$$X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1^{(1)} & \cdots & x_d^{(1)} \ \vdots & & & \vdots \ 1 & x_1^{(n)} & \cdots & x_d^{(n)} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} y^{(1)} \ \vdots \ y^{(n)} \end{bmatrix}$$

This single trick is why you'll see `np.hstack([X, np.ones((n,1))])` in basically every from-scratch linear regression implementation — it's not a hack, it's standard practice. Now all $n$ predictions happen in one matrix multiply: $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w}$. This _is_ the entire model. Everything else in this note is about finding the best $\mathbf{w}$.

## 2. When Should You Use Linear Regression?

Use it when:

- **You need a fast baseline.** Before trying anything complicated, fit a linear model. If it already gets you 90% of the way there, you've saved yourself weeks of work.
- **You need interpretability.** Each weight $w_j$ has a direct meaning: "holding everything else fixed, increasing feature $j$ by 1 unit changes the prediction by $w_j$." Doctors, regulators, and economists often _require_ this kind of transparency — a black-box neural net won't satisfy a clinical trial regulator.
- **The relationship is plausibly linear** (or can be made linear through feature engineering — see Section 10). Price per square foot, linear trends in time series, dose-response curves over a narrow range.
- **You have limited data.** Linear models have very few parameters ($d+1$), so they don't need much data to estimate reliably. A neural network with millions of parameters will overfit on 200 data points; linear regression won't.
- **You need a closed-form, no-training-loop solution.** The Normal Equation (Section 4) gives you the exact best answer through linear algebra — no learning rate tuning, no convergence worries.

Avoid it (or extend it) when:

- The true relationship is strongly nonlinear and you don't know the right transformation
- Your features have heavy multicollinearity and you need stable, unbiased coefficient _estimates_ (not just predictions) — use Ridge instead
- You have way more features than samples ($d \gg n$) — use Ridge/Lasso
- You need to model non-Gaussian, heavy-tailed, or bounded outputs (use GLMs, logistic regression, etc.)

## 3. How Do We Measure "Best"? The Loss Function

We need a number that tells us how wrong our predictions are, so we can search for the $\mathbf{w}$ that minimises it. The standard choice is **Mean Squared Error (MSE)**:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n}|\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}|^2 = \frac{1}{n}(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w})^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w})$$

In plain words: take the prediction error for every example, square it (so positive and negative errors don't cancel, and big errors get punished harder), and average.

**Why squared error specifically?** Three answers, in increasing depth — and conveniently, these three answers are also the three different _ways to derive_ the solution, covered next:

1. **It's geometrically natural.** Minimising MSE is exactly the same as finding the closest point to $\mathbf{y}$ that lives in the column space of $X$ — a _projection_.
2. **It's mathematically convenient.** It's smooth and differentiable everywhere, with a single, unique minimum (as long as $X$ has full column rank).
3. **It falls naturally out of probability theory.** If you assume Gaussian noise, minimising MSE is _mathematically identical_ to maximum likelihood estimation.

## 4. Three Derivations of the Same Answer

> [!tip] Why derive the same formula three different ways? Each lens teaches you something the others don't. Geometry gives you intuition you can _picture_. Calculus gives you the mechanical recipe you'd actually use to compute it. Probability tells you exactly _when_ squared error is the mathematically correct loss to use — and what to do instead if that assumption breaks.

### 4.1 Geometric View — Projection onto Column Space

The prediction vector $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\mathbf{w}$ is forced to live inside the **column space** of $X$, written $\text{col}(X)$ — meaning, no matter what $\mathbf{w}$ you pick, $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ can only ever be some combination of $X$'s columns. It can never be an arbitrary point in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

So the real question becomes: out of every point reachable inside $\text{col}(X)$, which one is _closest_ to the true $\mathbf{y}$?

The residual $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}$ is minimised exactly when it is **orthogonal** (perpendicular) to $\text{col}(X)$:

$$X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{0}$$

**Why must it be perpendicular at the minimum?** If the residual had _any_ component lying along a column of $X$, you could nudge $\mathbf{w}$ slightly in that direction and shrink the error further — meaning you hadn't actually reached the minimum yet. At the true minimum, there's no more "juice" left to squeeze along any direction $X$ is capable of moving in.

Solving the orthogonality condition above gives:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{w}^* = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}}$$

This is the **Normal Equation**. The matrix $P = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ is called the **projection matrix** (or **hat matrix**), so $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = P\mathbf{y}$ — predicting is literally just projecting $\mathbf{y}$ onto the space $X$ can reach.

```
        y
        |  \
        |    \  e (residual, orthogonal to col(X))
        |      \
col(X) ─────── ŷ = Pw
```

### 4.2 Calculus View — Minimise MSE Directly

This is the lens you'd actually use to _compute_ the answer by hand or in code.

Start by expanding the loss:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y} - 2\mathbf{w}^\top X^\top \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{w}^\top X^\top X \mathbf{w}\right)$$

Take the gradient with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ and set it to zero (the slope is flat at a minimum):

$$\nabla_\mathbf{w} \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{n}\left(-2X^\top\mathbf{y} + 2X^\top X\mathbf{w}\right) = \mathbf{0}$$

$$\Rightarrow \mathbf{w}^* = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y} \quad \checkmark$$

Same answer as the geometric view — which is reassuring, because it confirms the projection picture and the calculus aren't two different facts, they're two views of one fact.

> [!note] Matrix Calculus Rules Used
> 
> - $\nabla_\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{a}$
> - $\nabla_\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{w}^\top A\mathbf{w}) = 2A\mathbf{w}$ (when $A$ is symmetric)

### 4.3 Probabilistic View — MLE Under Gaussian Noise

This lens answers the deeper question: _why_ is squared error the "correct" loss in the first place, rather than an arbitrary convenient choice?

**Assume** each observation is generated as:

$$y^{(i)} = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(i)} + \epsilon^{(i)}, \qquad \epsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \text{ i.i.d.}$$

In words: the true relationship is exactly linear, but every observation gets a little random Gaussian noise added. Then:

$$p(y^{(i)} \mid \mathbf{x}^{(i)}; \mathbf{w}) = \mathcal{N}(y^{(i)}; \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, \sigma^2)$$

**Log-likelihood** of the entire dataset (using independence — the i.i.d. assumption lets us turn a product of probabilities into a sum of logs):

$$\ell(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y^{(i)} \mid \mathbf{x}^{(i)}; \mathbf{w}) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y^{(i)} - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}^{(i)})^2$$

The first term doesn't depend on $\mathbf{w}$ at all — it's a constant we can ignore. So **maximising** $\ell(\mathbf{w})$ is exactly the same problem as **minimising** $\sum_i(y^{(i)} - \hat{y}^{(i)})^2$.

$$\textbf{OLS} = \textbf{MLE under Gaussian noise}$$

> [!important] Key Insight The Gaussian noise assumption is _precisely_ what makes squared loss the mathematically "right" choice. This is not a coincidence and not a default — it's a direct consequence of a specific assumption about your data. If your noise were Laplacian instead of Gaussian, the MLE derivation above would instead produce $\ell^1$ loss (MAE) — giving you Least Absolute Deviations regression. The shape of your loss function is a statement about what kind of noise you believe is in your data.

## 5. The Normal Equation — Practical Considerations

The formula $\mathbf{w}^* = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$ looks clean on paper, but real datasets create real problems for it.

|Condition|Consequence|
|---|---|
|$X^\top X$ invertible (columns linearly independent)|Unique closed-form solution exists|
|$n < d$ (more features than samples)|$X^\top X$ is singular → infinitely many solutions, none uniquely "best"|
|Multicollinearity (correlated features)|$X^\top X$ is near-singular → numerically unstable, tiny data changes cause huge coefficient swings|
|$n$ very large|$O(d^3)$ matrix inversion becomes too slow → switch to gradient descent|

**Complexity breakdown:** forming $X^\top X$ costs $O(nd^2)$; inverting the resulting matrix costs $O(d^3)$. For small $d$ (a few thousand features), this is instant. Once $d$ climbs into the tens of thousands (common in NLP bag-of-words models, genomics, or image patches), $O(d^3)$ becomes painfully slow — that's exactly when Section 11's gradient descent takes over.

> [!warning] A practical numerical note In real code, never literally invert $(X^\top X)$ and multiply. Use a numerically stable solver instead — `np.linalg.lstsq` or `np.linalg.solve` — which avoids explicitly forming the inverse and is far more robust to near-singular matrices. The implementation in Section 12 follows this rule.

## 6. The Gauss-Markov Theorem — Why OLS Specifically?

Here's a question worth asking: out of _all_ possible ways to estimate $\mathbf{w}$ using a linear combination of the data, why is the Normal Equation's answer considered "the" standard one?

> [!theorem] Gauss-Markov Under the assumptions:
> 
> 1. $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0$ (errors average to zero, no systematic bias)
> 2. $\text{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma^2$ for all $i$ (homoscedasticity — constant noise variance)
> 3. $\text{Cov}(\epsilon_i, \epsilon_j) = 0$ for $i \neq j$ (errors are uncorrelated with each other)
> 
> the OLS estimator $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is **BLUE — Best Linear Unbiased Estimator.**

Formally: among every linear estimator $\tilde{\mathbf{w}}$ satisfying $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{w}}] = \mathbf{w}$ (i.e., also unbiased), OLS has the smallest possible variance for any linear combination of the coefficients.

This theorem does **not** require the noise to be Gaussian — only uncorrelated and equal-variance. Normality is a _separate, stronger_ assumption needed only if you want exact confidence intervals and hypothesis tests (recall Section 4.3's MLE derivation needed full Gaussianity; Gauss-Markov needs less).

> [!important] The catch worth remembering "Best among linear _unbiased_ estimators" is a narrower guarantee than it sounds. Ridge regression (Section 9) is _biased_ — yet it can still achieve lower total mean squared error than OLS. Gauss-Markov isn't violated here; it simply doesn't apply, because Ridge deliberately stepped outside the "unbiased" category. This is the bias-variance tradeoff in action: Ridge accepts a little bias in exchange for a meaningful reduction in variance, and that trade is often worth it.

## 7. Geometric Identities — The Algebra Behind the Picture

Recall $P = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$, the projection (hat) matrix from Section 4.1. It has several clean, provable properties:

|Property|Formula|Meaning|
|---|---|---|
|Idempotent|$P^2 = P$|Projecting twice does nothing new — once you're in $\text{col}(X)$, projecting again leaves you exactly where you are|
|Symmetric|$P^\top = P$|It's a genuine _orthogonal_ projection, not a skewed one|
|Annihilator|$M = I - P$|The complementary matrix — projects onto everything orthogonal to $\text{col}(X)$|
|$M\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{e}$|—|The residual is exactly the component of $\mathbf{y}$ that lives _outside_ $\text{col}(X)$ — the part the model fundamentally cannot reach|
|$\text{rank}(P) = d+1$|—|Matches the dimension of $\text{col}(X)$, i.e., the number of independent directions the model can predict along|

**Pythagorean decomposition.** Because $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and $\mathbf{e}$ are orthogonal by construction, the Pythagorean theorem applies directly to their lengths:

$$|\mathbf{y}|^2 = |\hat{\mathbf{y}}|^2 + |\mathbf{e}|^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{TSS} = \text{RSS} + \text{SSR}$$

(Total Sum of Squares = Regression Sum of Squares + Residual Sum of Squares — depending on textbook convention, the labels RSS/SSR sometimes swap, so always check which one means "explained" vs. "leftover error" in context.) This decomposition is exactly what powers the $R^2$ metric in the next section.

## 8. Evaluating the Model: $R^2$ and Diagnostics

### 8.1 $R^2$ — Fraction of Variance Explained

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}}{\text{TSS}} = 1 - \frac{|\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}|^2}{|\mathbf{y} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1}|^2}$$

- $R^2 = 1$: perfect fit — every bit of variance in $y$ is captured by the model.
- $R^2 = 0$: the model is no better than just always predicting the average of $y$.
- $R^2 < 0$: possible (and alarming) on a test set — it means the model is _worse_ than the trivial mean-predictor baseline. Strong sign of overfitting or a broken model.

> [!warning] $R^2$ pitfall $R^2$ on the _training_ set always increases (or stays the same) when you add more features — even completely useless, random ones. It cannot tell you whether those features generalise. Use **adjusted $R^2$** (which penalises extra features) or, far more reliably, evaluate $R^2$ on a genuine held-out test set.

### 8.2 Other Standard Metrics

- **MSE** ($\frac{1}{n}|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}|^2$): measured in _squared_ units of $y$. Heavily penalises large errors.
- **RMSE** ($\sqrt{\text{MSE}}$): back in the original units of $y$ — far easier to interpret ("on average we're off by about $X$").
- **MAE** ($\frac{1}{n}|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}|_1$): the average absolute error. Much more robust to outliers than MSE, because it never squares (and therefore amplifies) large errors.

### 8.3 Residual Plots — Your Most Honest Diagnostic Tool

Always plot predicted values $\hat{y}$ against residuals $\epsilon = y - \hat{y}$. The _shape_ of the scatter immediately tells you what's wrong, if anything:

```
Good (random scatter)     Heteroscedasticity      Nonlinearity
     ε                         ε                       ε
  •  •  •                   •     •  •              •      •
 • • • • •       vs.        •   •   ••     vs.     •  ••  •
  •  •  •                  •  •                  ••        ••
──────────── ŷ          ──────────── ŷ          ──────────── ŷ
```

- **Random scatter, no pattern:** good — your assumptions are roughly satisfied.
- **Funnel/fan shape:** heteroscedasticity (errors grow with $\hat{y}$) — Gauss-Markov condition 2 is violated. Consider transforming $y$ (e.g., log) or using weighted least squares.
- **Curved pattern:** nonlinearity — your core linearity assumption is violated. Consider polynomial features (Section 10) or a different model entirely.

## 9. Fighting Overfitting: Regularised Extensions

Plain OLS has a weakness: when features are correlated or you have many features relative to your data, it can produce wild, unstable coefficients that fit training noise perfectly but generalise badly. Regularisation fixes this by adding a penalty that discourages the weights from growing too large.

### 9.1 Ridge Regression (L2 Penalty)

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \underset{\mathbf{w}}{\arg\min} \ |\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}|^2 + \lambda|\mathbf{w}|^2$$

This still has a clean closed form:

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$$

The crucial bonus: $(X^\top X + \lambda I)$ is **always invertible** for any $\lambda > 0$, even when plain $X^\top X$ wasn't (recall Section 5's failure cases). This alone is a great reason to default to Ridge over plain OLS whenever you're unsure about multicollinearity.

**Effect:** shrinks every coefficient toward zero, smoothly, but never sets any of them to exactly zero.

**Probabilistic interpretation:** Ridge is exactly what you get from **MAP (Maximum A Posteriori) estimation** if you assume a Gaussian prior belief that weights should be small, $\mathbf{w} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2/\lambda \cdot I)$. In plain words: Ridge is what happens when you start with the belief "weights are probably small" and then update that belief using the data — the same MLE machinery from Section 4.3, just with an extra prior term bolted on.

### 9.2 Lasso Regression (L1 Penalty)

$$\mathbf{w}^* = \underset{\mathbf{w}}{\arg\min} \ |\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}|^2 + \lambda|\mathbf{w}|_1$$

There's no closed-form solution here, because the L1 penalty $|\mathbf{w}|_1 = \sum_j|w_j|$ is not differentiable at zero. It's solved numerically with coordinate descent or subgradient methods.

**Effect:** produces **sparse** solutions — many coefficients become _exactly_ zero. Lasso performs automatic feature selection: it tells you which features didn't matter, by zeroing them out entirely.

**Why does L1 create sparsity but L2 doesn't?** This is a genuinely satisfying geometric fact. Picture the loss function's contour lines (ellipses) trying to touch the boundary of the penalty region:

```
L2 constraint (circle)         L1 constraint (diamond)
      ___                            /\
    /     \                         /  \
   |       |                       /    \
   |       |        ←→            /      \
   |       |                      \      /
    \     /                        \    /
      ---                           \  /
                                      \/

Loss ellipse touches the           Loss ellipse very often
circle at some smooth, generic     touches the diamond at one
interior point (no coordinate       of its sharp corners — and
is forced to exactly zero)          corners sit on the axes,
                                     meaning some w_j = 0 exactly
```

A circle has no special points — the contact point could be anywhere. A diamond has _corners_, and those corners sit exactly on the axes. The loss is geometrically much more likely to first touch the diamond at a corner than anywhere else, and a corner means at least one coordinate is exactly zero.

**Probabilistic interpretation:** Lasso corresponds to MAP estimation with a Laplace prior, $w_j \sim \text{Laplace}(0, 1/\lambda)$ — a sharper, more peaked-at-zero prior than the Gaussian one Ridge uses, which is exactly why it pushes weights all the way to zero instead of just shrinking them.

### 9.3 Elastic Net (Both Penalties Together)

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{EN}}(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{n}|\mathbf{y}-X\mathbf{w}|^2 + \lambda_1|\mathbf{w}|_1 + \lambda_2|\mathbf{w}|^2$$

This combines L1's sparsity with L2's stability. It's preferred over pure Lasso specifically when features are highly correlated — Lasso alone tends to arbitrarily pick just one feature out of a correlated group and zero out the rest, which can be unstable; Elastic Net spreads weight more sensibly across the group.

### 9.4 Quick Reference Table

|Method|Penalty|Closed form?|Effect|Best used when|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|OLS|None|$(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$|Unbiased, but high variance if features are correlated|Clean, low-dimensional data|
|Ridge|$\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$|$(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$|Shrinks toward 0, never exactly 0|Correlated features|
|Lasso|$\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_1$|None (numerical solve)|Sparse — performs feature selection|High-dimensional, sparse true signal|
|Elastic Net|$\lambda_1\|\mathbf{w}\|_1 + \lambda_2\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$|None (numerical solve)|Sparse + stable|Correlated _and_ sparse features|

## 10. Bonus Trick: Polynomial Regression Is Still Linear Regression

This deserves to be called out explicitly because it trips people up: **fitting a curve does not require leaving the linear regression framework.**

Suppose $x \in \mathbb{R}$ (just one number), and you suspect the relationship is curved. Define new, engineered features:

$$\phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \ x \ x^2 \ x^3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Now fit $y = \mathbf{w}^\top\phi(x)$. Notice the model is still **linear in $\mathbf{w}$** — we never asked the optimiser to find a nonlinear combination of weights. We just handed it a richer set of input features ($x$, $x^2$, $x^3$) and let it find the best linear combination _of those_. Every tool from this note — the Normal Equation, gradient descent, Ridge, Lasso — applies completely unchanged.

The general lesson: **feature engineering means mapping $x \to \phi(x)$, then doing ordinary linear regression in $\phi$-space.** This exact idea, taken to its logical extreme (implicitly mapping into infinite-dimensional feature spaces without ever computing them explicitly), is the foundation of the kernel trick used in [[Kernel Ridge Regression]] and [[Support Vector Machines]].

```
Raw data (looks nonlinear)        Feature space (looks linear)
         *                                *
    *         *                      * *   * *
  *     |       *      ──→        *           *
    *         *                      * *   * *
         *                                *

Cannot fit with a straight line     Fits perfectly with a hyperplane
in x-space                          once you add x², x³, etc. as features
```

We also saw this exact pattern play out visually in Section 13's underfit/good-fit/overfit example — degree-1 features underfit, degree-2 matches the truth, degree-9 overfits.

## 11. Solving It at Scale: Gradient Descent

When $n$ or $d$ is large, the Normal Equation's $O(d^3)$ inversion is too expensive. Instead, we take small steps downhill on the loss surface until we land near the bottom.

### 11.1 The Gradient and Update Rule

From Section 4.2 we already have the gradient. The **batch gradient descent** update is:

$$\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \frac{\alpha}{n} X^\top(X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$$

Intuition: $X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}$ is the vector of current prediction errors. Multiplying by $X^\top$ sums up, for each weight, how much that weight's associated feature contributed to those errors across all examples — literally asking "if I increase weight $j$ slightly, does total error go up or down, and by how much?" Here $\alpha > 0$ is the **learning rate** — the size of each downhill step.

The single-sample version (**Stochastic Gradient Descent**, updating after just one example at a time) is:

$$\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \alpha\left(\hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\right)\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$$

> [!note] A name worth knowing This exact update rule predates modern deep learning entirely — it's the **Widrow-Hoff rule**, also called the **LMS (Least Mean Squares) update**, originally developed in 1960s adaptive signal processing. When you see "linear regression is the simplest neural network" later in Section 14, this is concretely _why_: the training rule used for adaptive filters six decades ago is identical to the SGD update used for a single linear neuron today.

### 11.2 Three Flavours of Gradient Descent

|Variant|Gradient computed on|Update frequency|Trade-off|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Batch GD**|Entire dataset|Once per full pass (epoch)|Exact gradient, very stable, but slow per step when $n$ is huge|
|**Stochastic GD (SGD)**|A single random example|Every example|Extremely noisy, but cheap and can sometimes escape bad local regions|
|**Mini-batch GD**|A small batch of size $B$|Every $B$ examples|The practical sweet spot — used everywhere in real ML|

A nice mathematical fact: the mini-batch gradient is an **unbiased estimator** of the true full-batch gradient. On average, the noisy mini-batch steps still point in the right overall direction.

### 11.3 Will It Actually Converge?

> [!note] Convergence Condition For batch GD, convergence is guaranteed when the learning rate satisfies $$\alpha < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}(X^\top X / n)}$$ where $\lambda_{\max}$ is the largest eigenvalue of $X^\top X/n$ (a formal bound on how curved the loss surface gets in its steepest direction). Too large a step relative to this bound and training will oscillate or diverge entirely; too small and training crawls.

### 11.4 Learning Rate Schedules

In practice, you rarely use a single fixed $\alpha$ for the whole training run:

- **Constant:** $\alpha_t = \alpha$. Simple, but can oscillate forever near the minimum instead of settling.
- **Step decay:** $\alpha_t = \alpha_0 \cdot \gamma^{\lfloor t/k \rfloor}$ — drop the learning rate by a factor every $k$ steps.
- **Cosine annealing:** $\alpha_t = \alpha_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{\max}-\alpha_{\min})\left(1+\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)\right)$ — smoothly glides the learning rate down following a cosine curve.
- **1-cycle:** warm up the learning rate first, then decay it — this specific schedule is widely used in modern deep learning training recipes.

## 12. Implementation (From Scratch, Both Methods)

```python
import numpy as np


class LinearRegression:
    """
    Linear regression fit by either the closed-form Normal Equation
    or batch gradient descent.

    Attributes:
        method: "normal" for closed-form solve, "gd" for gradient descent
        w: learned weight vector (last entry is the bias term)
    """

    def __init__(self, method: str = "normal"):
        self.method = method
        self.w = None

    def fit(self, X: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray) -> "LinearRegression":
        """X: (n, d) feature matrix: y: (n,) target vector."""
        n, d = X.shape
        X_aug = np.hstack([X, np.ones((n, 1))])  # (n, d+1), bias absorbed as last column

        if self.method == "normal":
            # w* = (XᵀX)^{-1} Xᵀy, but solved via lstsq for numerical stability
            # rather than literally forming and inverting (XᵀX).
            self.w, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(X_aug, y, rcond=None)

        elif self.method == "gd":
            self.w = np.zeros(d + 1)
            lr, epochs = 0.01, 1000
            for _ in range(epochs):
                grad = X_aug.T @ (X_aug @ self.w - y) / n
                self.w -= lr * grad
        else:
            raise ValueError(f"Unknown method: {self.method!r}")

        return self

    def predict(self, X: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        X_aug = np.hstack([X, np.ones((X.shape[0], 1))])
        return X_aug @ self.w

    def r2_score(self, X: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray) -> float:
        y_hat = self.predict(X)
        ss_res = np.sum((y - y_hat) ** 2)
        ss_tot = np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2)
        return 1 - ss_res / ss_tot


# --- Quick test ---
if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    X = rng.standard_normal((100, 3))
    true_w = np.array([1.5, -2.0, 0.8, 0.3])  # last entry is the bias
    y = np.hstack([X, np.ones((100, 1))]) @ true_w + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(100)

    model = LinearRegression(method="normal")
    model.fit(X, y)
    print(f"Estimated w: {model.w}")
    print(f"True w:      {true_w}")
    print(f"R²: {model.r2_score(X, y):.4f}")

    gd_model = LinearRegression(method="gd").fit(X, y)
    print(f"GD-estimated w: {gd_model.w}")
```

Both methods should recover weights very close to `[1.5, -2.0, 0.8, 0.3]`. The Normal Equation gets there in one shot; gradient descent gets there approximately, after enough epochs.

## 13. Worked Example: Watching Underfitting and Overfitting Happen

```python
import numpy as np

# Generate noisy quadratic data
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 100)
y = 0.5 * x**2 - x + 2 + rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=100)

def make_poly_features(x, degree):
    return np.column_stack([x**k for k in range(degree + 1)])

X1 = make_poly_features(x, 1)   # linear features: [1, x]
X2 = make_poly_features(x, 2)   # quadratic features: [1, x, x^2]  <- matches true data
X9 = make_poly_features(x, 9)   # degree-9 polynomial — way overkill

def fit(X, y):
    return np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]

w1, w2, w9 = fit(X1, y), fit(X2, y), fit(X9, y)

# degree=1 underfits (too simple to capture the curve)
# degree=2 is the correct fit (matches the true generating process)
# degree=9 overfits (chases every noisy wiggle in the training data)
```

```
Degree 1 (underfit)     Degree 2 (good fit)    Degree 9 (overfit)
        •                     •                      •
   •  ___  •              • ╭─╮ •                 ╭╮ •  ╭╮
 • /       \ •           • /   \ •              •╯  ╰─╯  ╰╮•
•/           \•         •/     \•              ╯           ╰
```

This single picture _is_ the bias-variance tradeoff, made concrete and visual. Degree 1 has high bias (it's structurally incapable of capturing the curve, no matter how much data you give it). Degree 9 has high variance (it latches onto the specific noise in this particular dataset; a different random sample would produce a wildly different wiggly curve). Degree 2 sits right at the sweet spot, because it matches the true underlying structure. See [[Bias Variance Tradeoff]] for the full mathematical derivation behind this picture.

## 14. Why This Is the Foundation of Neural Networks

This section is the payoff for everything above. It's why linear regression isn't "the boring beginner model" — it's the atom that every deep network is built from.

### 14.1 Linear Regression _Is_ a Neural Network (the Simplest One)

A linear regression model with a single output is, structurally, a neural network with zero hidden layers and a linear (i.e., "do nothing") activation function:

```
Input layer          Output
  x_1 ──── w_1 ───┐
  x_2 ──── w_2 ───┤──→ ŷ = w^Tx + b
   ⋮              ⋮
  x_d ──── w_d ───┘
```

Every major idea from this note carries straight over into deep learning:

- **Gradient descent** (Section 11) is the literal training algorithm for every neural network ever trained, from this 4-weight toy model to GPT-scale transformers.
- **Regularisation** (Section 9) fights overfitting in deep networks exactly the same way it does here.
- **MSE loss** is still the default loss for any regression _head_ sitting at the end of a deep network — for example, predicting a house price or a continuous sensor value.
- **The Normal Equation** even shows up directly in practice: when fine-tuning a pretrained network by only retraining its final linear layer (a common transfer-learning trick), that layer can sometimes be solved for in closed form, exactly as in Section 4.

### 14.2 Feature Learning: The Real Difference Between Classical ML and Deep Learning

In classical machine learning, _you_ hand-design the features $\phi(x)$ (Section 10) and then run linear regression on top of them.

In deep learning, the network _learns_ $\phi$ from raw data automatically, through its hidden layers. But notice: the very last layer of the network is still nothing more than a linear model sitting on top of whatever features got learned:

$$\hat{y} = W_{\text{out}} \cdot \underbrace{\phi_\theta(x)}_{\text{learned representation}} + b$$

Training end-to-end jointly optimises the feature extractor $\phi_\theta$ (everything except the last layer) _and_ the linear head $W_{\text{out}}$ at the same time, via backpropagation. This is the single biggest conceptual reason linear regression deserves a deep, not cursory, understanding: it is literally the output stage of every regression-style deep network in existence.

### 14.3 Mini-Batch SGD Here = Backpropagation There

The mini-batch update rule from Section 11.1 is _exactly_ a forward pass followed by a backward pass through a single-layer network. There is no extra machinery hiding inside "backpropagation" for this simple case — it's the same chain rule, applied once. Internalising this equation first is genuinely the best way to demystify backprop in deeper networks later: you're not learning something new, you're learning to apply this same idea repeatedly through more layers.

### 14.4 Weight Decay Is Ridge Regression in Disguise

In deep learning frameworks, adding L2 regularisation to _every_ weight in the network is called **weight decay** (e.g., setting `weight_decay=1e-4` in a PyTorch optimiser). This is not a vaguely similar idea — it is mathematically the exact same penalty as Ridge regression (Section 9.1), and it carries the exact same Bayesian interpretation: a Gaussian prior pulling every weight gently toward zero.

### 14.5 Three Lenses on the Same Equation

|Field|OLS is seen as...|The goal|
|---|---|---|
|Classical Statistics|An _estimator_ with known statistical properties (Gauss-Markov, Section 6)|Inference — confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, "is this effect real?"|
|Machine Learning|A _model_ trained by minimising a loss|Prediction — minimise error on new, unseen data|
|Deep Learning|The _final linear layer_ sitting on top of learned features|Representation learning + prediction, jointly optimised|

## 15. The Assumptions Underneath Linear Regression

Linear regression is the mathematically _optimal_ choice specifically when these hold:

1. **Linearity:** the true relationship is genuinely $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x] = \mathbf{w}^\top x$, or at least close to it.
2. **Independence:** training examples are drawn independently of each other (no example "leaks" information about another).
3. **Homoscedasticity:** the noise variance $\text{Var}(\epsilon^{(i)}) = \sigma^2$ stays constant — it doesn't grow or shrink as $x$ changes (this is Gauss-Markov condition 2 from Section 6).
4. **No multicollinearity:** the columns of $X$ are linearly independent (Section 5).
5. **Normally distributed residuals:** needed only for formal statistical _inference_ (confidence intervals, p-values) — not needed for plain prediction. This is the assumption Section 4.3's MLE derivation depends on, but Gauss-Markov (Section 6) does not.

> **A practical note worth remembering:** linear regression turns out to be surprisingly forgiving of violations to assumptions 3 and 5 — predictions can still be decent even when residuals aren't perfectly normal or constant-variance. What actually breaks the model is a genuine violation of assumption 1 (the relationship truly isn't linear) or a severe violation of assumption 4 (your feature matrix is nearly singular). The single best diagnostic habit: **always plot your residuals** (Section 8.3).

## 16. Failure Modes and Fixes — Quick Reference

```
Problem                  →  Symptom                     →  Fix
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Overfitting (high d)     →  Low train, high test error   →  Ridge / Lasso regularisation
Multicollinearity        →  Huge, unstable coefficients  →  Ridge (L2), PCA preprocessing
Non-linearity             →  Systematic residual pattern  →  Feature engineering / kernels
Heteroscedasticity        →  Residuals fan out             →  WLS or transform y
Outliers                  →  MSE pulled heavily            →  Huber loss / LAD regression
n << d                    →  Singular XᵀX                  →  L1/L2 penalty, dimensionality reduction
```

## 17. Drawbacks and Limitations — The Honest Accounting

Knowing _when not_ to reach for this model is as valuable as knowing how to build it.

- **It cannot capture genuine nonlinearity** without manual feature engineering. If the true relationship has curves, interactions, or thresholds you haven't explicitly encoded as features, the model will systematically underfit — no amount of additional data fixes this, because the error is bias, not variance (see [[Statistical Learning Theory]]).
    
- **It's fragile to outliers.** Because the loss squares every error, one extreme outlier can drag the entire fitted line toward it, distorting predictions for every other point. (MAE-based regression, motivated by Laplacian-noise MLE in Section 4.3, is more robust here, at the cost of losing the clean closed-form solution.)
    
- **Multicollinearity destroys coefficient stability.** Even if predictions stay reasonable, the _individual_ weights can become wildly unstable and uninterpretable when features are highly correlated — small changes in the data can flip a coefficient's sign entirely. This is dangerous if you're using the weights for interpretation rather than just prediction.
    
- **It assumes constant-variance, independent noise** (Gauss-Markov, Section 6). Real-world data — especially time series, panel data, or anything with measurement scales that grow — frequently violates this. Ignoring it doesn't crash the model, but it does invalidate any confidence intervals or p-values you compute.
    
- **It scales poorly to very high dimensions without regularisation.** With $d \gg n$, plain OLS has no unique solution at all (Section 5) — you are mathematically required to add a penalty (Ridge/Lasso) or reduce dimensionality first.
    
- **It cannot represent interactions or thresholds implicitly.** If feature A only matters when feature B exceeds some value, plain linear regression has no way to express that unless you manually construct an interaction feature ($x_A \cdot x_B$) — the model has zero ability to discover such structure on its own.
    
- **It offers no built-in uncertainty about individual predictions** beyond what classical statistical theory provides under fairly strict assumptions (Section 15) — unlike, say, Bayesian or ensemble approaches that naturally produce predictive uncertainty.
    
- **Gradient descent variants introduce their own failure modes** — a poorly chosen learning rate (outside the bound in Section 11.3) can cause divergence, and on genuinely huge feature counts, even forming gradients can be expensive without further engineering (sparse representations, feature hashing, etc.).
    

None of these are reasons to avoid linear regression. They're reasons to know precisely _when_ its assumptions hold well enough to trust it, and when to reach instead for Ridge/Lasso, polynomial features, or a fundamentally different model class.
